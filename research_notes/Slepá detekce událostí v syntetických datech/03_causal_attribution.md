# Causal attribution among overlapping business events (frequency drop vs. churn, primary vs. downstream, per-segment controls)

Scope: methods for the blind detective. Miss (b): segment churn that follows a price change is dated 5–6 weeks early, because the price change first lowers purchase frequency and the gap-based churn onset reads that as leaving. False finds come from side effects of big real events (substitution, rebounds). Constraints: Python, under 60 s, blind, explainable to students, a DiD-style number per event.

Note on sources: every item under "Cited Findings" has a link. Items under "Inferences" are my own reasoning or standard textbook knowledge that I did not re-check against a source in this session. They are marked as such so the writer can judge them.

## (1) Telling "buys less often" from "has left" when there is no exit signal (BTYD, survival / hazard)

### Takeaway
Latent-attrition (BTYD) models separate a purchase rate λ from a dropout process, so "buys less often" and "has left" are different parameters by construction. A Pareto/NBD with time-varying covariates (Bachmann, Meierer & Näf 2021) lets a price-change dummy act on the purchase rate and the attrition rate separately. In Python under 60 s, the practical version is a cheap MLE BG/NBD fit per segment on rolling calibration windows, plus a discrete-time hazard logit with a "price-exposed" covariate. These turn "the gap grew" into "λ fell at t0, while P(alive) only starts dropping at t1". The full Bayesian or covariate models are R-only or too slow.

### Cited Findings
**Model families**
- Pareto/NBD (Schmittlein, Morrison & Colombo 1987) is the reference latent-attrition model. BG/NBD keeps its overall structure but changes the assumed dropout story: a customer can drop out after each purchase, with a geometric probability. That makes it much easier to estimate. The authors say Pareto/NBD's "empirical application can be challenging, especially in terms of parameter estimation". Fader, Hardie & Lee, *Marketing Science* 24(2), 2005, DOI 10.1287/mksc.1040.0098 — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:24:y:2005:i:2:p:275-284); [preprint PDF](https://www.brucehardie.com/papers/bgnbd_2004-04-20.pdf)
- The R package BTYD implements BG/BB, BG/NBD and Pareto/NBD (data prep, estimation, scoring) — [CRAN BTYD refman](https://archive.linux.duke.edu/cran/web/packages/BTYD/refman/BTYD.html)
- **Time-varying covariates:** Bachmann, Meierer & Näf (2021), "The Role of Time-Varying Contextual Factors in Latent Attrition Models for Customer Base Analysis", *Marketing Science* 40(4):783–809, DOI 10.1287/mksc.2020.1254. It is a closed-form MLE extension of Pareto/NBD with time-invariant and time-varying contextual factors, which "can influence either the purchase or the attrition process, or both". On three retail datasets it gave a significant gain in predicting future customer activity — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v40y2021i4p783-809.html); [open PDF (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/395075264.pdf)
- That extension lives in the R package CLVTools (v0.12.1, Nov 2025; authors include Bachmann, Meierer, Näf). The package also has BG/NBD and GGom/NBD, plus regularisation and equality constraints on covariate effects — [CRAN CLVTools](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/CLVTools/index.html); [Meierer et al. 2026, arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- In one applied report, the Pareto/NBD with time-varying covariates in CLVTools was computationally expensive, and convergence depended on the optimiser, the starting values and how homogeneous the customers were. This is a single student report, not a benchmark — [CMU Tepper retail-chain report](https://www.cmu.edu/tepper/recruiting-and-partnerships/partner-with-the-tepper-school/assets/images/retail-chain.pdf)
- **Pareto/GGG** (Platzer & Reutterer 2016, *Marketing Science*, "Ticking Away the Moments: Timing Regularity Helps to Better Predict Customer Activity"). It adds a per-customer regularity parameter, so inter-purchase times follow a Gamma distribution rather than an exponential. It is estimated by MCMC, it is in R BTYDplus (`demo("pareto-ggg")`), and it has no covariate support there. Reutterer et al. 2020 (IJRM) proposed the analytic (M)BG/CNBD-k as an "easy way" alternative — [BTYDplus README](https://github.com/schumzy/btydplus); [arXiv 2608.19022 (amortised VI for non-Poisson BTYD)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19022)
- **Hidden Markov alternative:** Netzer, Lattin & Srinivasan (2008), *Marketing Science* 27(2):185–204, is a non-homogeneous HMM over latent relationship states (in their application: dormant / occasional / active). Encounters move customers between states, and the model is estimated with hierarchical Bayes. It is conceptually the "less often vs. gone" split as states — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v27y2008i2p185-204.html); [Stanford GSB page](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/hidden-markov-model-customer-relationship-dynamics)

**Python implementations**
- `lifetimes` was archived by its owner on 28 Jun 2024 (read-only, "won't be adding new features, improvements, or even answering issues"). The README names PyMC-Marketing as its successor — [lifetimes GitHub](https://github.com/CamDavidsonPilon/lifetimes)
- PyMC-Marketing has `BetaGeoModel` (BG/NBD) and `ParetoNBDModel`. They take a frequency / recency / T table, and the notebooks show estimates matching the `lifetimes` MLE on CDNOW — [BetaGeoModel API](https://pymc-marketing.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/generated/pymc_marketing.clv.models.beta_geo.BetaGeoModel.html); [Pareto/NBD notebook](https://pymc-marketing.readthedocs.io/en/latest/notebooks/clv/pareto_nbd.html); [BG/NBD notebook](https://pymc-marketing.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/clv/bg_nbd.html)

**Survival / hazard**
- lifelines `CoxTimeVaryingFitter` needs "long format" data: one row per interval (id, start exclusive, stop inclusive) for each covariate change. `fit(..., start_col, stop_col, id_col, weights_col, robust)`. The helper `lifelines.utils.add_covariate_to_timeline` folds covariate changes into the base table — [lifelines time-varying docs](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Time%20varying%20survival%20regression.html); [CoxTimeVaryingFitter API](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/fitters/regression/CoxTimeVaryingFitter.html)
- Andersen–Gill recurrent-event model: uses the total-time (counting-process) scale and assumes one common baseline hazard across successive spells — [arXiv 2505.01044 tutorial on recurrent-event Cox subtypes](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01044); [PMC4387664](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4387664/)
- I found no source saying lifelines has a dedicated Andersen–Gill class. AG in lifelines would be built by hand as long-format counting-process rows with `id_col` and `robust=True`. That is an inference from the API, not documented — [lifelines docs](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Time%20varying%20survival%20regression.html)

### Inferences
- **Why this fixes miss (b):** the gap-based onset sees "inter-purchase time went up" and calls it churn. A model with a separate λ and dropout reads a uniform slowdown of all segment members as a fall in λ: the surviving customers still buy, just more slowly. Leaving shows up as a growing share of customers with zero orders past a gap that is long *relative to their own new, lower rate*. Proposed blind recipe, per segment (about 300 series → aggregated to ~10–30 segments):
  1. **Rate step:** detect a change in the segment's mean orders per active customer per week with `ruptures` (PELT/Binseg) or a Poisson GLM with a step. This gives t_rate.
  2. **Re-baseline the gap:** after t_rate, each customer's "overdue" threshold is k × (their *post-t_rate* expected inter-purchase time), for example the 95th percentile of an exponential or Gamma with the new rate, rather than the pre-period rate. Churn onset t_churn is then the first week where the share of overdue customers departs from control. This alone should move the student churn onset roughly 5–6 weeks later, because the old threshold fires as soon as the rate falls.
  3. **Report** two DiD numbers per segment: Δ frequency among survivors (intensive margin) and Δ share alive (extensive margin).
- **P(alive) per customer** from BG/NBD has a closed form: P(alive | x, t_x, T) = 1 / (1 + δ_{x>0} · a/(b+x−1) · ((α+T)/(α+t_x))^{r+x}). This formula is from the Fader–Hardie–Lee paper; I did not re-verify it in this session. Averaging it by segment over rolling "as-of" dates T gives a P(alive) time series. A dated churn onset is the changepoint of that series. Each fit is 4 parameters on a few thousand rows (scipy `minimize`, milliseconds), so 20–40 rolling dates × 20 segments fits well inside the 60 s budget. Caveat: with fixed parameters, P(alive) still treats a slowdown as partial death. Refit after t_rate, or give the dropout and purchase rate a price-exposure covariate (Bachmann 2021). Only the Bachmann covariate version does this in closed form, and it is R-only.
- **Discrete-time hazard (recommended as the explainable workhorse):** person-week rows; logit P(order in week w | alive) and, separately, logit P(final order in week w), with the final order defined in hindsight by a long gap at the end of the data. Covariates: segment × post-price, segment × post-event, week fixed effects. This is statsmodels GLM, fast with DuckDB aggregation, and the coefficients read as DiD odds-ratios. The two equations split the slowdown (order hazard) from the exit hazard. This is a competing-risks-style split: "last purchase" and "next purchase" are competing outcomes of each interval.
- Use Cox / AG (lifelines) when per-customer inter-purchase-time hazards with time-varying `post_price` are wanted. Expect seconds rather than milliseconds on ~10⁶ interval rows; it is feasible but less needed.
- PyMC-Marketing NUTS sampling per segment and per rolling date would probably exceed 60 s. Use MLE (custom scipy) for the detective and keep PyMC for offline explanation.

## (2) Separating a primary effect from downstream consequences (cascades, substitution, rebounds)

### Takeaway
The literature options range from full causal discovery (PC / PCMCI, AIOps root cause analysis) to rule-based explaining-away. Benchmarks show causal discovery struggles with edge direction on large graphs. For about 10–50 detected anomalies, the best fit to the constraints is a small explicit cascade layer:
- (a) temporal precedence with a lag window;
- (b) a known causal template, such as supply → stock-out → "sold out" tickets → substitution, or courier → late delivery → rating → churn;
- (c) a sign / mass-balance rule, where an opposite-sign shift in a neighbouring category starting when a stock-out starts is substitution;
- (d) a conditional ("mediated") re-test: refit the downstream DiD controlling for the upstream series. If the effect vanishes, mark it as a consequence.

### Cited Findings
- **PCMCI** (Runge et al. 2019, *Science Advances* 5(11) eaau4996) combines linear or non-linear conditional-independence tests with a PC-style algorithm to estimate lagged causal networks from large time-series datasets. PCMCI+ adds contemporaneous links. It is implemented in the Python package Tigramite — [PMC6881151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6881151); [arXiv 1702.07007](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.07007v2); [survey arXiv 2303.15027](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.15027)
- **AIOps root cause analysis:** CausalRCA learns a weighted causal graph by gradient-based structure learning and then ranks root causes with PageRank. RCD (Ikram et al.) learns only the part of the graph near the root cause, which avoids many conditional-independence tests. CIRCA uses a graph from domain knowledge with regression-based hypothesis testing. RUN uses neural Granger + PageRank — [arXiv 2408.13729 (benchmark)](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13729v1); [CausalRCA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/166189852/CausalRCA.pdf); [RUN arXiv 2402.01140](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01140)
- A benchmark of 9 causal discovery methods and 21 root-cause methods found that "all common causal discovery methods have difficulties in working with large graphs and estimating edge directions" — [Pham et al., arXiv 2408.13729](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13729v1)
- DynaCausal argues that ranking by per-service anomaly score causes misattribution: the services that deviate most are wrongly prioritised over the real root cause. This is exactly the "large downstream side effect beats a small primary" failure — [arXiv 2510.22613](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22613v1)
- CIRCA's design (a structural graph from domain knowledge + regression-based hypothesis testing) is the closest published analogue to "a known event template + a conditional re-test" — [arXiv 2408.13729](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13729v1)

### Inferences
- **Recommended rule set (explainable, blind, O(n²) over a few dozen candidates):**
  1. **Precedence:** candidate B is a *possible consequence* of A if onset_B ∈ [onset_A, onset_A + L], with L about 0–8 weeks, *and* B is in a plausible downstream channel of A. Channels are known from the schema, not from the answer key: inventory → tickets(topic=sold out) → orders in other categories; courier → delivery delay → review rating → orders/subscriptions; city quality → cancellations.
  2. **Substitution rule:** a stock-out / negative shock in category C, plus a positive shift in neighbouring category C′ in the same segment or city with the same onset (±1 week), where the C′ gain is ≤ the C loss in units or revenue, gives C′ = substitution. Suppress C′ or attach it to C.
  3. **Rebound rule:** a positive anomaly right after a negative one in the same series, with a similar size (the lost mass is recovered: stockpiling or delayed demand), gives a rebound. Attach it to the parent event.
  4. **Mediation re-test (explaining away):** refit B's DiD with the upstream mediator as a covariate, for example weekly late-delivery share for ratings, or stock-out days for substitution. If B's effect shrinks by more than ~50 % or loses significance, it is downstream. This is the regression analogue of conditional independence (B ⊥ A | M), and it is what PC / PCMCI does automatically. A Baron–Kenny-style product-of-coefficients split gives students a "direct vs. indirect" number. I did not search for a mediation-analysis source.
  5. **Lagged cross-correlation / Granger** (statsmodels `grangercausalitytests`) between candidate series only as a tie-breaker for direction. Short series (~95 weeks) give low power.
- Full PCMCI over ~300 series × 95 weeks is feasible in Tigramite with ParCorr (likely seconds to tens of seconds), but the edge directions are unreliable according to the benchmark above. Running it only on the 10–30 flagged anomaly series is the cheap way to get an "explaining-away" check.
- Most relevant to the false finds: rules 2–4, because substitution and rebounds come from a *known mechanism* that an explicit template captures better than generic discovery.

## (3) Better counterfactual controls than "the rest of the population"

### Takeaway
Replace "the rest" with a weighted donor pool fitted on pre-period trajectories. Options: synthetic control (Abadie), Augmented SCM (ridge bias-correction for poor pre-fit), or Synthetic DiD (unit and time weights, DiD-style intercept, which gives the per-event DiD number the constraints ask for). All three are a small quadratic problem per treated series, so ~300 series is easily under 60 s. BSTS / CausalImpact is slower and less transparent. Donor-pool contamination by spillovers (substitution into neighbouring categories, competitor-affected cities) is the main risk. Exclude donors flagged as downstream by section (2), or use spillover-aware SCM.

### Cited Findings
- Abadie (2021), "Using Synthetic Controls: Feasibility, Data Requirements, and Methodological Aspects", *JEL* 59(2):391–425. It gives practical guidance and lists contextual and data requirements on which validity depends — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:59:y:2021:i:2:p:391-425); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/144417)
- **Augmented SCM** (Ben-Michael, Feller & Rothstein, *JASA* 116(536):1789–1803, 2021). When SCM cannot fit the pre-period well, it uses an outcome model (ridge) to estimate the bias from imperfect pre-fit and de-bias the SCM estimate, while limiting extrapolation from the convex hull. R package augsynth — [arXiv 1811.04170](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04170); [NBER w28885](https://nber.org/papers/w28885)
- **Synthetic DiD** (Arkhangelsky, Athey, Hirshberg, Imbens & Wager, *AER* 2021) is a panel estimator built from DiD and SC, reported as more robust than either. The theory assumes latent unit × time factors. The reference implementation is R `synth-inference/synthdid`. A third-party Python package `sdid` is on PyPI (MIT; I could not verify its maintenance) — [arXiv 1812.09970](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.09970); [NBER w25532](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25532.pdf); [PyPI sdid](https://pypi.org/project/sdid/)
- **pysyncon** (Python, v1.5.1) implements standard SCM, robust SCM (Amjad, Shah & Shen 2018), Augmented SCM (ridge, matching augsynth `progfunc="Ridge"`) and penalised SCM (Abadie & L'Hour 2021). It also has placebo tests and confidence intervals, and it aims to reproduce R Synth/augsynth — [GitHub pysyncon](https://github.com/sdfordham/pysyncon); [augsynth docs](https://sdfordham.github.io/pysyncon/augsynth.html)
- **CausalImpact / BSTS** (Brodersen et al., *Annals of Applied Statistics* 9:247–274, 2015). Python ports:
  - `tfcausalimpact` (TensorFlow Probability; variational inference by default for speed);
  - Google's `tfp-causalimpact` (not an officially supported product);
  - `pycausalimpact`, which uses a statsmodels Kalman filter (classical, not Bayesian), so results can differ from R.
  — [PyPI tfcausalimpact](https://pypi.org/project/tfcausalimpact); [PyPI tfp-causalimpact](https://pypi.python.org/project/tfp-causalimpact/0.1.0/); [PyPI pycausalimpact](https://pypi.org/project/pycausalimpact)
- **Spillover-contaminated donors:** Cao & Dowd (arXiv 1902.07343, revised Jan 2026) estimate the direct and spillover effects together under an assumed linear spillover structure. They argue that contaminated units are often central to building the synthetic control, so dropping them can hurt, and that a "pure donor" approach has large worst-case bias when the spillover structure is wrong. Implemented as SPILLSYNTH in `mlsynth` — [arXiv 1902.07343](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.07343); [mlsynth SPILLSYNTH docs](https://mlsynth.readthedocs.io/en/latest/spillsynth.html)
- He, Li, Shi & Miao (arXiv 2411.01249) use a two-stage factor-model + robust regression that needs only coarse information on how wide the interference is. Melnychuk (arXiv 2405.01645) compares alternatives, including Inclusive SCM (Di Stefano & Mellace) and iteratively replacing affected donors with their synthetic versions — [arXiv 2411.01249](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01249v1); [arXiv 2405.01645](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.01645)

### Inferences
- **Recommended default: SDID-style or penalised SCM written by hand in numpy/scipy.** Unit weights come from non-negative least squares (or `scipy.optimize` with simplex constraints) of the treated segment's pre-period weekly series on donor segments, with an intercept, which is what makes it DiD-like. Time weights cover the pre-period weeks. The estimate is a weighted DiD, so it keeps the "DiD-style number" and is explainable as "a weighted average of similar segments plus a level shift". The per-series cost is a small QP (donors ≲ 300, pre-weeks ≲ 90), milliseconds each, so 300 series take a few seconds. Placebo-in-time z can stay, and placebo-in-space (each donor in turn as fake treated) becomes available.
- Augmented SCM (pysyncon) is useful when the treated segment sits outside the donors' convex hull, for example the largest city or the student segment with unique seasonality.
- CausalImpact per series with VI is plausibly seconds per series, so ~300 series would probably break the 60 s budget. Use it only on the shortlisted candidates, if at all. This estimate is not benchmarked.
- **Contamination handling for this database:**
  - (i) build donor pools *within the same dimension* (segment vs. segments, city vs. cities, category vs. categories);
  - (ii) exclude donors in the same "neighbourhood" as the treated unit: substitute categories for a stock-out, same-courier cities for a courier switch;
  - (iii) exclude donors that have their own flagged anomaly overlapping the window;
  - (iv) report the estimate with and without suspect donors as a robustness check. Cao & Dowd warn that exclusion can cost fit, so show both.
- For miss (b) specifically, a synthetic control on the *share alive* (as defined in (1)) rather than on order counts should date the extensive-margin onset against an untreated-segment counterfactual. The price change hits all segments' frequency, but only students leave, so the donor segments absorb the common frequency drop and leave the student-specific exit visible.
